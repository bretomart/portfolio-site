## Introduction

We, the delegation of Germany, reaffirm our strong support for the European Commission’s Green Deal
as a cornerstone of the European Union’s path toward climate neutrality and sustainable economic
growth. While Germany has at times been viewed as a Western “climate laggard,” we recognize the
pressing need to accelerate our transition away from fossil fuels. Our current energy mix still relies on
oil, coal, and natural gas, yet substantial progress has been made in expanding renewable energy
generation and improving energy efficiency across all major sectors. For us, the Green Deal is not only
an environmental necessity but also an economic opportunity to modernize transport, agriculture,
industry, and the building sector, securing long-term prosperity and competitiveness.

As a member of the Green Growth Group, Germany advocates for a collective increase in the EU’s 2030
carbon emission reduction target to 55% compared to 1990 levels. We believe that climate ambition
must be coupled with cohesion, ensuring that no member state or region is left behind in the transition
process. To achieve this, we support a substantial Just Transition Fund (JTF), designed to alleviate social
and economic disparities arising from decarbonisation. However, we stress that such support should
remain conditional on measurable climate progress and transparent implementation to maintain both
fairness and effectiveness across the Union.

We also underline the importance of flexibility in achieving climate neutrality. We view natural gas as a
necessary transitional energy source to guarantee stability and affordability during the gradual coal
phase-out, provided it is fully phased out by 2050. We maintain that nuclear energy remains
incompatible with our national energy strategy. We consider it unsafe, unsustainable, and economically
inefficient, and therefore oppose directing EU funds toward constructing nuclear power plants.


## Agenda


## 2030 Carbon Emission Targets

We, the delegation of Germany, reaffirm our commitment to the establishing of European Union as the
first climate-neutral continent by 2050. At the core of this vision lies the legally binding 55% greenhouse
gas reduction target by 2030, compared to 1990 levels, as outlined in the EU Climate Law (Clean Energy
Wire, 2025). Germany’s own Climate Protection Act (Klimaschutzgesetz) mirrors this ambition,
introducing binding annual emission budgets across the energy, transport, buildings, and industrial
sectors (Umweltbundesamt, 2025).

Recent data from the German Environment Agency shows that emissions fell by 3.4% in 2024, reaching
649 million tonnes of CO₂ equivalents, placing Germany broadly on track to achieve its 2030 objectives if
policy consistency is maintained (Umweltbundesamt, 2025). Independent assessments by Climate
Action Tracker and Reuters rate Germany’s climate policy as “almost sufficient”, noting that short-term
goals are achievable but that post-2030 progress must accelerate to sustain momentum (Climate Action
Tracker, 2025; Reuters, 2025).

Domestically, Germany recognises that its energy mix must continue to evolve. While progress in
renewable energy deployment has been substantial, oil, coal, and natural gas still play a limited
transitional role.



## The Just Transition Fund (JTF)

We, the delegation of Germany, reaffirm our strong support for the Just Transition Fund (JTF) as a
cornerstone of the European Union’s commitment to ensuring that no region or citizen is left behind in
the shift toward a climate-neutral economy (European Commission, n.d.). The Fund, established under
Regulation (EU) 2021/1056, provides essential financial assistance to territories most affected by the
transition away from fossil fuels, helping them diversify their economies, invest in clean technologies,
and reskill their workforce (EPRS, 2021).

We remain one of the largest contributors and beneficiaries of the JTF, with allocations exceeding €4
billion, including national co-financing (Clean Energy Wire, 2024). Our eligible regions, North Rhine-
Westphalia, Saxony, Saxony-Anhalt, and Brandenburg are undergoing profound structural changes
linked to the national coal phase-out by 2038 (EPRS, 2021). The approval of Germany’s Territorial Just
Transition Plans by the European Commission in 2022 reaffirmed our shared commitment to sustainable
regional development and green innovation (European Commission, n.d.).


## The Role of Natural Gas

We, the delegation of Germany, recognise the continuing debate within the European Green Deal
framework concerning the role of natural gas as a transitional energy source. Natural gas can serve as a
temporary bridge fuel, supporting the phase-out of coal and the expansion of renewable capacity across
the continent, provided that its use remains strictly limited in duration (Clean Energy Wire, 2024). The
country has achieved substantial emissions reductions as renewables now account for more than half of
total electricity generation (Umweltbundesamt, 2025).

However, persistent challenges in the transport and buildings sectors underscore the need for reliable
back-up capacity during the transition (Reuters, 2025). Natural gas therefore plays a stabilising role, 
ensuring continuity of supply and economic balance while renewable systems and storage technologies
continue to expand (DNV, 2025). We, the delegation also emphasizes the importance of forward-looking
infrastructure, advocating for hydrogen-ready systems that prevent technological lock-in and facilitate
the eventual replacement of gas with clean alternatives (International Energy Agency, 2025).


## The Role of Nuclear Energy

We, the delegation of Germany, reaffirm our long-standing position that nuclear energy should not form
part of Europe’s long-term climate strategy. Our approach remains shaped by the post-Fukushima
consensus and the commitment to a renewable-based energy transition. Germany’s nuclear phase-out
was completed on 15 April 2023, when the final power plants, Isar 2, Emsland, and Neckarwestheim 2
were permanently shut down under the Atomic Energy Act, marking the end of more than six decades of
nuclear generation (Federal Office for the Safety of Nuclear Waste Management [BASE], 2023).

The Atomausstieg policy reflects a societal and political agreement that the risks of nuclear power its
safety concerns, waste management challenges, and financial burden outweigh its potential benefits.
The Federal Environment Ministry (BMUV) continues to stress that the phase-out was a necessary step
to remove “unmanageable risks” and to accelerate renewable energy deployment (BMUV, 2021). By
2024, this policy direction yielded record renewable output and the lowest coal use in six decades,
countering early predictions of energy shortages (World Nuclear Industry Status Report, 2024).


At the European level, we recognise that opinions differ on whether nuclear energy should be classified
as a “green” investment under the EU guidelines. While we maintain our anti-nuclear stance, we
acknowledge that certain Member States, such as France and Sweden, continue to rely on nuclear
energy for their energy security (European Relations, 2025). For this reason, we support the
establishment of a working group on nuclear safety and waste management during the negotiations.
This ensures that cooperation continues safety and decommissioning, even where national energy
choices diverge.


## Decision-Making Procedure

The decision-making procedure applied during the negotiations followed the principle of unanimity
voting. This method required that all participating member states reach a common agreement before
any proposal or amendment could be adopted. The unanimity rule reflects the intergovernmental
nature of the Council’s decision-making in matters related to climate and energy policy, where national
interests and sovereignty considerations remain significant.

Within the simulation, unanimity ensured that all delegations regardless of size or economic weight had
equal influence over the outcome. This approach promoted compromise and dialogue, compelling
states to find balanced solutions that accommodated differing national priorities, such as levels of
energy dependency, transition costs, and domestic climate ambitions. Although unanimity can slow
down the negotiation process, it reinforces the legitimacy and collective ownership of the final
agreement.


## Hard Issues and Exchange Strategy

The primary hard issues for the German delegation during the negotiation concerned the Just Transition
Fund (JTF) and the role of nuclear energy. The JTF represented a significant point of negotiation.
Germany strongly supported solidarity with coal-dependent and less developed member states but
insisted on accountability and measurable progress. The difficulty arose in reconciling the need for
financial support with the assurance that EU funds would be used efficiently and transparently. While
Germany was willing to increase the Fund to €35 billion without resistance, this was contingent upon
strengthening the Commission’s monitoring authority and introducing clear spending rules to prevent
misuse.

The role of natural gas was a moderate issue. Germany viewed it as a necessary transitional fuel, vital
for maintaining energy stability during the coal phase-out, but firmly supported its phase-out by 2050.
The most difficult issue remained nuclear energy. We maintain a clear opposition to new nuclear
investments, citing safety risks, waste disposal challenges, and incompatibility with renewable-based
energy systems. However, we recognize the realities faced by countries like Sweden and Finland, which
rely heavily on nuclear power. To avoid deadlock, we opted to support a compromise allowing
continued operation of existing plants until 2050, alongside increased investment in renewable energy
and the establishment of a working group on nuclear safety and waste storage.



## Linkages Between Agenda Issues and Germany’s Position

We, the delegation of Germany recognizes that the 2030 carbon emission target is directly connected to
our commitment to achieving a 55% reduction in greenhouse gas emissions across the EU by 2030. This
target reflects Germany’s ambition to strengthen the Union’s collective climate policy while maintaining
fairness and feasibility for all member states. The objective aligns with Germany’s national Climate
Protection Act and its broader strategy of balancing environmental ambition with social and economic
stability.

The Just Transition Fund is another agenda item firmly tied to our position. The Fund represents a vital
tool to ensure that no region or citizen is left behind during the energy transition. Germany’s support for
expanding the JTF to €35 billion, with reinforced Commission monitoring and stricter spending rules,
demonstrates its belief that solidarity must go hand in hand with accountability. The role of natural gas
is moderately linked to our perspective as well. The delegation of Germany recognizes the necessity of
gas as a transitional energy source to guarantee security of supply while renewable energy capacity
expands. The role of nuclear energy, however, cannot be directly linked to our policy priorities.
Germany’s national and political consensus rejects nuclear energy as a sustainable solution, given the
associated safety risks, waste management problems, and incompatibility with a decentralized
renewable system. While we respect other member states’ energy choices, it does not view nuclear
power as part of its own transition path.


## Level-Playing Field: Allies and Adversaries

We, the delegation of Germany has identified potential partners and opposition towards
our plans.


## Potential Supporters

    1. Green-oriented and committed climate states

            o   Countries like Denmark, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, and the Nordic states tend to
                push for ambitious 2030 targets and favor strong environmental governance.

            o   These states are generally skeptical of nuclear and less reliant on heavy fossil sectors,
                making them natural allies on rejecting new nuclear and emphasizing renewables.

    2. Coal-dependent or transition-needing states that require support, but want conditions

            o   Poland, Romania, and Bulgaria might support a larger Just Transition Fund, given their
                structural challenges, though they may resist strict conditionality.

            o   Some Central European countries that still depend on gas may accept our transitional
                gas proposal, if phase-out by 2050 is guaranteed.

    3. Moderate states wary of locking into nuclear or fossil dependency

            o   Spain, Italy, and Portugal may prefer flexibility but lean toward renewables over nuclear.
                They may support our cautious stance on nuclear and gas if complemented by robust
                funding and governance.


            o   France (traditionally pro-nuclear) is a tricky case while often opposed to our outright
                rejection of nuclear, recent trends suggest some convergence, possibly making limited
                agreements feasible on transitional compromises.


## Likely Opponents or Difficult Negotiating Partners

    1. Pro-nuclear member states

            o   France is the central champion of nuclear power in the EU. It will likely oppose our
                resistance to EU support for nuclear, pressing instead for inclusion of nuclear in EU
                funding and favorable classification.

            o   Eastern European states with existing nuclear fleets or plans (e.g. Slovakia, Czech
                Republic, Hungary) will resist strong anti-nuclear stances and may block agreements
                that restrict nuclear’s role.

    2. States heavily dependent on fossil fuels or gas infrastructure

            o   Countries with significant reliance on gas (e.g. in Southeastern Europe or those with
                legacy gas infrastructure) may resist aggressive phase-out timelines or conditional
                limitations on gas use.

            o   Some (“laggard”) states may oppose stricter monitoring or conditional use of JTF funds,
                viewing them as threats to sovereignty or national discretion over spending.


## Opening Strategy, Coalitions, and Negotiation Dynamics

We, the delegation of Germany aimed to establish credibility early in the debate. We emphasized
cooperation, accountability, and ambition, positioning itself as a constructive yet principled actor within
the Council setting. By articulating specific parameters for the emission target and JTF conditionality, we
wanted to signal readiness to negotiate details while maintaining clear boundaries on issues such as
nuclear expansion.

Due to time constraints and limited negotiation rounds, our use of coalitions was restricted. The only
effective partnership we were able to establish was with Spain, whose position aligned closely on
renewable energy priorities and caution toward nuclear expansion. This cooperation proved useful in
reinforcing mutual support for stricter oversight mechanisms within the JTF and promoting renewable
investment as the preferred path toward transition.

The European Commission also served as a valuable ally, given our insistence on enhanced monitoring
powers and rule-based spending within EU funds. The relationship with the Presidency was neutral and
procedural. We did not develop a direct working relationship or seek preferential influence.


## Evaluation: Minimum Objectives and Non-Negotiable Issues

We, the delegation of Germany, evaluate the outcome of the negotiations as a balanced and
constructive agreement that successfully upholds the core objectives of the European Green Deal. The
final package reaffirmed the 55% emission reduction target by 2030, the expansion of a conditional and


well-monitored Just Transition Fund, and a clearly defined transitional role for natural gas, all of which
align closely with Germany’s initial negotiation goals.

The Just Transition Fund (JTF) emerged as a key success of the negotiations. We had strongly advocated
for an increase of the Fund to €35 billion, combined with enhanced Commission monitoring powers
and transparent spending mechanisms. The final agreement integrated these principles, securing a
stronger link between solidarity and accountability.

On the issue of natural gas, we had achieved our principal objective: the recognition of gas solely as a
temporary transitional fuel within the EU’s energy mix. The final agreement confirmed that its use
would be limited to the transition period and fully phased out by 2050. The most sensitive issue during
the negotiations was nuclear energy. We upheld its long-standing position that no EU funds should be
allocated to new nuclear projects. While acknowledging that some Member States rely on existing
nuclear infrastructure, the outcome established that investments would focus on the transition and
safety management of existing plants, rather than their expansion.
