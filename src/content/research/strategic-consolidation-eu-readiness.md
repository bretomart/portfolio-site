Strategic Consolidation or Normative Erosion: Assessing the European
Union's Internal Readiness for a New Global Era

## The Geopolitical Dilemma: Readiness, Disillusionment, and the Transactional Turn

The contemporary discourse surrounding the readiness of the European Union has reached a
state of intense apprehension, animated by shifting global alliances, heightened systemic
competition, and profound transatlantic uncertainty. Historically anchored in a self-conception of
normative power, the Union has long positioned itself as a regulatory superpower governed by
democratic values, multilateralism, and the rule of law. However, contemporary geopolitical
shocks have induced a hasty transition toward reactive realpolitik. Facing periodic electoral
volatility within the United States, alongside the rapid electoral ascension of populist factions
across European member states, EU leadership has begun justifying compromises of
foundational norms under the mantle of pragmatic adaptability. Rather than undertaking the
arduous structural reforms required to resolve internal systemic vulnerabilities, policymaking has
increasingly defaulted to short-term transactionalism.

This strategic retreat misidentifies the true nature of European readiness. The electoral shifts
observed across member states cannot be dissociated from pervasive institutional
disillusionment. Citizens perceiving supranational institutions as remote, technocratic, and
ineffective in addressing localized socio-economic strain have progressively turned toward
reactionary alternatives. Instead of resolving this disconnect through institutional revitalization
and domestic capacity building, European policymakers have sought superficial victories
abroad. They have pursued fragile accords with authoritarian regimes, outsourced critical legal
responsibilities, and sustained fragmented commercial dependencies. This trajectory
demonstrates a fundamental priority misalignment. Attempting to purchase geopolitical
resilience through unprincipled concessions surrenders the normative core that justifies
European integration. True readiness does not emanate from opportunistic externalization, but
from internal institutional consolidation, principled sovereignty, and democratic legitimacy across
the European continent.

The tension between declared normative commitments and transactional enforcement is most
acutely demonstrated in the evolution of European migration policy. Faced with domestic
political pressures and border securitization demands, the Union has systematically shifted its
focus from coherent internal asylum harmonization to the aggressive externalization of border
control. The legislative progression of European return policy, crystallized in the compromise
text of Council Document 10363/26, codifies this shift into binding regulatory law. By
establishing a common system for the return of third-country nationals, this framework explicitly
subordinates humanitarian considerations to the imperative of expedited removal.

The operational mechanics of this regulation reveal the extent to which the Union is willing to
compromise its legal identity to achieve temporary logistical deterrence. Under Article 17 of the
proposal, member states are empowered to conclude return agreements with third countries for
the transfer and stay of individuals who possess no nationality or established connection to
those destination states, relying solely on nominal guarantees that international human rights


standards and non-refoulement will be respected. This framework facilitates the creation of
offshore processing and detention architecture, allowing member states to transfer individuals to
non-European jurisdictions that frequently lack judicial oversight. Furthermore, Article 37a
cements this transactional turn by formalizing the use of external conditionality, demanding that
development aid, preferential trade access, and visa policies be instrumentalized to compel
third-country compliance with readmission mandates.

This externalization strategy represents a profound structural error. By contracting migration
enforcement out to autocratic regimes, the European Union compromises its moral authority
while granting authoritarian leaders coercive leverage over European domestic politics. These
arrangements provide no durable stability, as external transit regimes consistently utilize
migration flows as geopolitical extortion levers to extract diplomatic concessions and sustained
financial transfers. Simultaneously, this policy neglects internal governance. Domestically,
European institutions impose restrictive funding constraints and bureaucratic hurdles on civil
society organizations and municipal bodies that facilitate authentic community integration and
humanitarian care. Rather than confronting systemic administrative deficits within member
states through transparent legal channels, the Union embraces the methods of illiberalism to
achieve the illusion of border control.

A parallel dynamic of strategic vulnerability is evident in the European Union's economic and
infrastructural posture toward systemic competitors. While European leadership routinely
employs the rhetoric of economic derisking and open strategic autonomy, historical investment
patterns illustrate a chronic failure of collective industrial cohesion. The empirical mapping
conducted by Casagrande and Dallago (2025) provides clear evidence of this structural
fragmentation, detailing how the proliferation of China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) and the
Digital Silk Road (DSR) exploited the institutional fissures of the continent.

Analyzing 727 projects across 46 European states, Casagrande and Dallago demonstrate the
emergence of a polarized two-speed Europe that directly reflects internal disparities. In
Southern and Eastern Europe, where public capital was severely constrained by post-crisis
austerity measures, Chinese state-owned enterprises acquired strategic ownership stakes in
foundational logistics hubs, such as the Greek Port of Piraeus, and financed high-risk physical
transit corridors across the Western Balkans. Conversely, Western and Northern European
economies aggressively integrated Chinese high-tech investments into their digital
infrastructure, with approximately 72 percent of all documented projects falling under the Digital
Silk Road in advanced telecommunications, smart city networks, and data centers.

This dynamic revealed an absence of unified strategic planning. When geopolitical friction and
cybersecurity concerns subsequently compelled sudden regulatory prohibitions, member states
were confronted with massive decommissioning expenses and delayed technological
deployments, epitomized by the disjointed removal of equipment in the United Kingdom.
Commercial relations with external powers ought to remain functional, arm's length transactions
rather than deep, uncontrolled dependencies. The vulnerability of the European Union was
never an inevitable consequence of global trade, but the direct result of an internal failure to


fund common industrial champions, secure indigenous microchip fabrication facilities, and
coordinate continental technology procurement.

In attempting to compensate for these internal structural deficiencies, European foreign policy
frequently retreats into performative global expansion. A prominent manifestation of this
tendency is the recurring political inclination to float association agreements or
quasi-membership constructs with geographically distant partners, such as Canada. Presenting
transoceanic partners as potential associate members dilutes the constitutional identity of the
Union. The European Union is inherently a continental project grounded in geographic
contiguity, shared legal frameworks, and deep economic harmonisation. Blurring these
geographic boundaries transforms the Union from a cohesive constitutional polity into a
disjointed global trade forum, distracting leadership from the pressing imperative of continental
consolidation.

A corresponding failure of strategic foresight characterizes Europe's reactive militarization under
the framework of transatlantic defense requirements. Spurred by political rhetoric from the
United States regarding mandatory North Atlantic Treaty Organization expenditure benchmarks,
European states have engaged in rapid budget inflations centered primarily on the procurement
of off-the-shelf foreign defense materiel. This posture confuses military expenditure with
authentic strategic autonomy. Escalating military posturing without independent European
industrial production lines simply deepens reliance on the American defense apparatus.
American presidential administrations remain subject to the fluctuating mandates of their
domestic electorate, operating within political cycles that cannot guarantee long-term strategic
constancy for external alliances. Committing extensive European sovereign wealth to external
procurement pipelines to placate transient political figures who may not remain in power over
the coming decade represents reckless, short-sighted governance. Furthermore, uncoordinated
military accumulation prioritizes defensive posturing over diplomatic de-escalation, consuming
vital capital that could otherwise be deployed to resolve the economic instability and
infrastructure deficits driving domestic political alienation across the Union.

If Europe's house is to be set in order for a volatile global era, the Union must replace
reactionary externalization with systematic domestic consolidation. In the realm of migration
management, distribution and reception cannot remain an exercise in authoritarian outsourcing.
Instead, the allocation of asylum seekers must be placed under transparent domestic
institutional oversight, incorporating rigorous, objective feasibility assessments analogous to the
statutory review functions exercised by the Council of State (Raad van State) in the
Netherlands. Municipalities must not have arbitrary quotas imposed upon them without
institutional verification; rather, assignments must be formally evaluated against localized
absorption capacities, available housing supply, and medical infrastructure. Direct European
funding must flow into local municipal structures and civic integration organizations, eliminating
the administrative chokeholds that currently undermine community stability.

Simultaneously, the Union must assert genuine economic independence. Rather than
squandering resources on fragmented external procurement, capital must be aggregated into


common European industrial programs focused on strategic capabilities, particularly domestic
semiconductor manufacturing, clean energy generation, and sovereign digital infrastructure.
Commercial interaction with external markets, whether concerning Chinese manufacturing or
South American trade blocs, must remain disciplined and transactional. European markets must
be governed by stringent reciprocity mandates, ensuring that foreign state capitalism cannot
exploit regulatory divergences between member states.

Ultimately, resilience requires confronting the root cause of domestic disillusionment. The Union
cannot sustain external authority while projecting an internal image of paralysis, perceived by its
citizens as an entity restricted to passive monitoring missions and declaratory statements.
Supranational governance must demonstrate tangible efficacy that improves everyday
livelihoods, accompanied by proactive educational integration. Civic awareness regarding
European institutional mechanisms must be embedded as a fundamental, standardized
curriculum across national school systems incentivizing robust voter turnout in European
elections. Rebuilding democratic trust from within member state populations is the ultimate
prerequisite for European power. Only by securing its constitutional boundaries, modernizing its
internal industrial base, and refusing to barter foundational democratic principles for transient
geopolitical reprieve can the European Union establish true internal readiness and stand as an
uncompromised global force.
