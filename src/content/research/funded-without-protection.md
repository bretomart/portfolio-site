## Abstract

In this paper, we will discuss and ask: **how does the politicization of migration disproportionately undermine the very support frameworks built to manage its human consequences even when those frameworks address problems that political actors themselves claim to care about?** The central case is CMMA (Coordination Center on Human Trafficking and Migrant Smuggling Aruba), an antitrafficking and migrant support agency in Aruba drawn from the author's own volunteering and interview material with its director, Jeanette Richardson, and its coordinator of victim assistance, Suekyen. CMMA's funding has been destabilized from three directions at once: the termination of U.S. international NGO support in 2025 support withdrawn from precisely the kind of antitrafficking work the U.S. rhetorically prioritizes, a Kingdom-level border security grant ending this year with no confirmed successor, and an uncertain Aruba government budget contribution repeatedly deprioritized against competing fiscal pressures.

This funding collapse was not the last of the structural pressures: it was compounded weeks later by the January 2026 U.S. military intervention in Venezuela, which intensified displacement pressure across the Dutch Caribbean at the exact moment CMMA's capacity to respond had already been cut. The paper argues that this is not simply underfunding, but a specific pattern in which political priorities rather than a genuine absence of resources determine which frameworks receive stable, structural budgeting and which are left to survive on informal lobbying and ad hoc grants. It further argues that this pattern is structurally enabled by the Kingdom of the Netherlands' own constitutional architecture: Aruba's status as an EU associated Overseas Country and Territory extends EU citizenship and EU linked funding eligibility to the island, while neither Dutch nor EU asylum law actually applies there.

Drawing on Wimmer and Glick Schiller's critique of methodological nationalism, Mezzadra and Neilson's account of the border as a productive rather than neutral mechanism, and Quijano's, Samaddar's, and Bhambra's accounts of coloniality and the racialized politicization of migration, the paper situates CMMA's precarity as a structural, not incidental, feature of how the Kingdom and the EU jointly govern this territory.

As a brief comparative illustration, the paper closes by drawing on a supplementary interview with the R&D leadership of Senbis, a Dutch polymer technology company, whose EU funded sustainability project is fully and securely resourced but whose realworld impact is nonetheless undercut by political disinformation and a lack of policy appetite for the standards that would let its benefits reach the public. This brief parallel is used only to show that political interference undermining broadly beneficial work is not unique to migration governance while keeping the paper's central concern on the specifically racialized and postcolonial dynamics that shape migration support frameworks in particular.

## Research Question and Sub-Questions

**Main question:** How does the politicization of migration disproportionately undermine the support frameworks built to manage it, even where those frameworks address problems political actors claim to prioritize?

**Subquestions:**

1. What do CMMA's three simultaneous funding losses (U.S. NGO support, the Kingdom border security grant, the uncertain Aruba budget contribution) reveal about political priority setting rather than genuine resource scarcity and what does the timing relative to the January 2026 U.S. intervention in Venezuela add to that picture?
    
2. How does the Kingdom of the Netherlands' constitutional structure (the Kingdom Charter, OCT status, EU citizenship without EU or Dutch asylum law) actively produce, rather than passively contain, CMMA's precarity?
    
3. What are the lived, day-to-day institutional consequences of this precarity for CMMA's staff informal lobbying in place of formal budgeting, bureaucratic bottlenecks (CENSO, AZV), and staffing insecurity?
    
4. (Brief, comparative) Does a structurally similar pattern political disinterest or disinformation undermining a broadly beneficial initiative appear outside migration governance, as in the Senbis case, and what does that suggest about how politicization functions as a general tool against evidence-based, beneficial work, even as migration governance carries additional racialized and postcolonial dynamics that this comparison does not capture?

## Object of Analysis / Empirical Material

1. **CMMA (Aruba)**: Primary case based upon approximately 40 hours of volunteering and fieldwork, including:
   - Interview material with Director Jeanette Richardson and Coordinator Suekyen (utilizing 7S framework interviews);
   - Triple funding precarity: Kingdom-level border security grant ending with no confirmed replacement, collapsed U.S. international NGO funding following 2025 cuts, and an uncertain Aruba government budget contribution amid competing fiscal pressures;
   - The January 2026 U.S. intervention in Venezuela as a compounding structural shock, intensifying displacement pressure precisely as operational response capacity was curtailed;
   - Institutional strain documentation: two-person administrative team, coordinator contract vulnerability, bureaucratic bottlenecks (CENSO registration, AZV medical access), and reliance upon informal political lobbying;
   - Geopolitical and reputational stakes: TIP Tier 2 status monitoring, downgrade vulnerability, and GRETA evaluations.

2. **Structural and Legal Context**: The Kingdom Charter, the Overseas Countries and Territories (OCT) status of Aruba, Curaçao, and Sint Maarten, the institutional divergence between European Union citizenship eligibility and the non-application of European Union and Dutch asylum law, alongside Amnesty International's 2024 findings on Aruban asylum procedures.

3. **Senbis (Emmen, Netherlands)**: Brief comparative illustration drawn from supplementary interviews with R&D leadership regarding the European Union Just Transition Fund "Biotech" consortium (Senbis, TNO, and University of Groningen) developing biodegradable drop-in replacement textile polymers. While fully and securely funded, real-world deployment faces headwinds from political disinformation and absence of public certification standards. This parallel illustrates political interference in evidence-based systems outside migration policy.

## Preliminary Theoretical Framework

- **Wimmer & Glick Schiller (2002)** on methodological nationalism: No single jurisdiction (Aruba, the Kingdom of the Netherlands, or the European Union) assumes structural responsibility for CMMA, resulting in accountability being diffused where protection is required.
    
- **Mezzadra & Neilson (2013)** on the border as method: The OCT and European Union boundary is not a passive sorting mechanism, but an active producer of institutional categories with direct material consequences for CMMA's operational mandate.
    
- **Quijano (2000) / Samaddar (2020)** on the coloniality of power and postcolonial migration governance: The Kingdom Charter functions as an active constitutional apparatus that allocates resources and rights according to historical colonial boundaries.
    
- **Bhambra (2017)** on methodological whiteness: Demonstrates how political rhetoric celebrating anti-trafficking priorities can coexist with, and mask, the material withdrawal of resources necessary for operational survival.
    
- Note on Senbis: This comparative parallel resides outside the postcolonial migration toolkit, illustrating industrial disinformation rather than colonial governance, and serves strictly illustrative purposes.

## Preliminary Structure

2. **Theoretical Framework**: Methodological nationalism, border as method, coloniality of power, and methodological whiteness.

   - 3.4 Institutional consequences: Navigating operational hurdles without structural public budgeting.

5. **Discussion**: Distinguishing factors unique to Caribbean migration governance (racialization, coloniality, Kingdom Charter structure) versus broader systemic patterns in political treatment of evidence-based work.
    
6. **Conclusion**: Synthesis of institutional findings and implications for postcolonial governance frameworks.
