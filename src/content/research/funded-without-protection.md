## 1. Introduction

Forced displacement, cross-border mobility, and the political rhetoric surrounding displaced
populations represent defining governance challenges across the Western Hemisphere. While
regional absorption is predominantly analyzed through continental receiving states like
Colombia, its most acute demographic manifestation occurs across the island territories of the
Southern Caribbean. On the autonomous island of Aruba, located twenty-four kilometers off the
Venezuelan coast, the ratio of displaced persons reaches approximately 159 per 1,000
residents, representing the highest per-capita displacement density recorded globally. Despite
the routine integration of Venezuelan nationals within daily Aruban society, the structural
mechanics of their journey, the precarious nature of their legal existence, and the systemic
barriers embedded in state institutions remain largely obscured in public and political discourse.

The central argument of this paper is that the politicization of migration produces a fundamental
misalignment between the high-profile commitments governments make to combat human
trafficking and the material resources they actually allocate to frontline protection. Political actors
frequently claim to prioritize anti-trafficking enforcement, yet their budgetary and administrative
decisions systematically undermine the small institutions tasked with doing the work. In the
Dutch Caribbean, this misalignment is not an accident of local mismanagement. It is structurally
produced by the constitutional architecture of the Kingdom of the Netherlands, which links
islands like Aruba to European funding and citizenship while withholding the legal protections of
European asylum law.


The paper develops this argument in five steps. First, it establishes an integrated theoretical
framework drawing on Wimmer and Glick Schiller’s critique of methodological nationalism,
Mezzadra and Neilson’s concept of border as method, and postcolonial critiques from Quijano,
Samaddar, and Bhambra. Second, it examines the constitutional mechanics of the Kingdom
Charter and the Overseas Countries and Territories (OCT) framework. Third, it analyzes CMMA
as a frontline case study, tracing how simultaneous funding collapses and the January 2026
U.S. military intervention in Venezuela exacerbated the agency’s precarity. Fourth, it
deconstructs the 2025 Trafficking in Persons (TIP) data to demonstrate the visibility paradox in
official metrics. Finally, it examines counter-discourse strategies through youth educational
outreach and reflects on the methodological limits of practitioner-grounded research.


## 2. Theoretical Framework and Operational Synthesis


### 2.1 Methodological Nationalism and Border as Method

To understand migration governance on a small Caribbean island, one must first dismantle the
assumption that the nation-state is the natural container of society and politics. Andreas
Wimmer and Nina Glick Schiller describe methodological nationalism as the tendency of social
science and policy to treat nation-state borders as self-evident containers of social processes.
When applied to the Southern Caribbean, a methodological nationalist perspective treats
Venezuelan displacement as either "Venezuela's internal crisis" or "Aruba's domestic
management problem". That framing completely obscures the reality: the legal rules, border
funding, and diplomatic stances governing this migration corridor are split across Washington,
Brussels, The Hague, and Oranjestad simultaneously.

Sandro Mezzadra and Brett Neilson extend this critique through their concept of "border as
method". They argue that borders are not passive perimeters that merely separate territories;
they are active, productive devices that stratify labor, create differential legal statuses, and
determine who receives protection. In the Dutch Caribbean, the 27-kilometer maritime strait
between Venezuela and Aruba is not just a geographic boundary. The legal line separating full
European Union territory from EU-associated OCT territory actively produces precarious
categories of existence: migrants who are physically adjacent to Europe and governed by Dutch
sovereign authority, but legally barred from European asylum standards.


### 2.2 Coloniality of Power and Methodological Whiteness

The distribution of these borders is not arbitrary. Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the "coloniality of
power" and Ranabir Samaddar’s postcolonial analysis of migration illustrate how imperial
hierarchies persist in modern constitutional arrangements. The Kingdom Charter (Statuut) acts
as a living continuity of colonial-era governance. It concentrates sovereign competencies
(defense and foreign affairs) within the European center in The Hague, while leaving the social,
financial, and humanitarian consequences of regional displacement entirely to the autonomous
Caribbean countries.

Gurminder Bhambra’s critique of "methodological whiteness" explains the discursive mechanism
that sustains this division. Methodological whiteness describes how dominant political and


academic frameworks obscure racialized power dynamics while professing universal values. In
anti-trafficking governance, this explains how states maintain high-profile rhetoric about human
rights, trauma-informed care, and victim protection, while systematically withholding the material
and legal resources required to protect racialized, non-European migrants on their peripheries.


### 2.3 Integrated Theoretical Synthesis

Rather than treating these scholars as separate checkpoints in a literature review, they operate
together as a single explanatory sequence. The chain begins with Wimmer and Glick Schiller's
critique of methodological nationalism, which reveals how political actors across Washington,
The Hague, and Oranjestad fragment authority and evade structural responsibility by viewing
cross-border movement through narrow state boundaries.

This fragmented jurisdiction enables what Mezzadra and Neilson term the border as method:
the boundary between the European Union and its associated Overseas Countries and
Territories does not just divide territory, but actively produces administrative limbo by channeling
resources into border interdiction while denying institutional protection to the people crossing.

Finally, Quijano and Samaddar's coloniality of power, combined with Bhambra's methodological
whiteness, explain why this divide falls where it does. These frameworks demonstrate how
postcolonial constitutional structures continue to offload humanitarian costs to the Caribbean
periphery, allowing political centers to maintain a rhetoric of human rights in international forums
while systematically withholding the material resources needed to protect racialized migrants on
the ground.


## 3. Constitutional Architecture and the OCT Protection

      Gap

### 3.1 The Kingdom Charter and OCT Status

The Kingdom of the Netherlands is a single sovereign state under international law comprising
four constituent countries: the Netherlands, Aruba, Curaçao, and Sint Maarten. Under the 1954
Kingdom Charter (Statuut voor het Koninkrijk der Nederlanden), these Caribbean countries
possess internal autonomy, maintaining their own parliaments, tax regimes, and civil
administrations. However, defense and foreign relations remain Kingdom affairs governed from
The Hague.

Under European Union law, Aruba holds the status of an Overseas Country and Territory (OCT).
Arubans hold Dutch passports, making them full European Union citizens with the legal right to
live, work, and move freely throughout the European continent. Furthermore, European
development and security funds flow into the island, including border-control subsidies and
regional integration grants.




### 3.2 The Asylum and Protection Asymmetry



Despite this constitutional connection to Europe, the legal architecture of protection stops at the
water's edge. Neither Dutch domestic asylum law nor the EU Common European Asylum
System (CEAS), including the EU Qualification Directive, applies in Aruba. The EU Qualification
Directive establishes minimum standards for subsidiary protection, recognizing that individuals
fleeing generalized violence, armed conflict, or systemic humanitarian collapse require
international protection even if they do not meet the individual persecution standard.

Because the Qualification Directive does not bind Aruba, the island's domestic legal framework
is anchored strictly in a narrow reading of the 1951 Refugee Convention. Aruba recognizes
asylum claims almost exclusively on the basis of direct political persecution. In practice, roughly
98 percent of Venezuelan migrants arriving on the island are fleeing generalized economic
collapse, lack of medicine, starvation, and systemic deprivation. Because the local legal
apparatus has no category for humanitarian or economic displacement, these claims are
classified as economic, leaving nearly the entire arriving population in an irregular legal status
without statutory avenues for regular stay.

The Dutch government and European institutions actively support maritime interdiction and
border enforcement in the Caribbean, but they treat migrant reception and protection as strictly
local Aruban responsibilities. European sovereignty and security are projected outward, but
European human rights protections remain contained on the continent.


## 4. Institutional Case Study: CMMA


### 4.1 The Two-Person Reality and Mandate

The Coördinatiecentrum Mensenhandel Mensensmokkel Aruba (CMMA) is Aruba’s dedicated
national coordinating body for human trafficking and migrant smuggling. Structurally positioned
under the Aruban Ministry of Justice, the agency is tasked with executing a massive mandate
organized around three operational pillars:

   ●​ Information: Documenting trends, gathering intelligence, managing data, and raising
      public awareness.​

   ●​ Education: Training frontline actors across law enforcement, healthcare, hospitality, and
      education.​

   ●​ Assistance: Coordinating medical care, shelter, legal aid, psychosocial support, and
      documentation for identified victims.

Despite this formal ministerial portfolio, CMMA functions in practice as a two-person agency:
Director Jeanette Richardson and Victim Assistance Coordinator Sukyuen. In an institutional
interview, Richardson summarized their operational capacity plainly: "We're doing the job of
entire departments with two people". They operate out of a small office designed for two desks,
with no dedicated government shelter for victims, no direct operational budget line, and no
structural staff buffer.



### 4.2 The Triple Funding Cliff

Between 2025 and 2026, CMMA was struck by three simultaneous funding collapses, exposing
the fragility of non-structural budgeting.

The first instance came into view with the abrupt termination of United States funding for
international non-governmental organizations across the Caribbean in 2025. Implementing
partners such as HIAS, the International Organization for Migration (IOM), and the Pan
American Development Foundation (PADF) had historically provided direct victim assistance in
Aruba, including food vouchers, emergency housing, and legal support. When these
organizations were forced to shut their local operations, CMMA had to absorb the care burden
with no replacement funding.

This was followed immediately by the scheduled expiration of Project Versterking Grenstoezicht,
the Dutch Kingdom border-security grant that had financed CMMA's core operational
campaigns, educational modules, and administrative expenses.

Finally, Aruban national budget contributions offered no safety net. While the government
covered base staff salaries through the National Central Bureau of Counterterrorism,
Intelligence, and Security (NCTVI), it provided no dedicated line item for direct victim assistance.
In parliamentary budget debates, supplementary funding for CMMA was repeatedly pushed
aside in favor of general cost-of-living adjustments and fiscal crisis measures, leaving frontline
protection entirely dependent on in-kind resources and ad-hoc improvisations.


### 4.3 Geopolitical Shockwaves: Operation Absolute Resolve

This fiscal contraction was compounded on January 3, 2026, when the United States launched
Operation Absolute Resolve, a military strike in Caracas that captured Nicolás Maduro and flew
him to New York to face federal drug trafficking indictments. The geopolitical rupture was felt
instantly in the Dutch Caribbean. Regional airspace was shut down, commercial flights were
halted, and Queen Beatrix Airport in Aruba sat frozen during the peak tourism season.

While political rhetoric in Washington declared Venezuela "liberated," the regional migration
reality became more volatile. The U.S. administration terminated Temporary Protected Status
(TPS) designations for over 600,000 Venezuelans and froze asylum processing, generating
immediate return and deportation pressures across the diaspora. In the Southern Caribbean,
maritime borders remained closed, direct air links were non-existent, and smuggling routes
became more dangerous. Rather than triggering mass voluntary return, the intervention
heightened the precarity of displaced Venezuelans, increasing their vulnerability to human
trafficking and exploitation at the exact moment CMMA's institutional resources were at their
lowest ebb.



### 4.4 Frontline Institutional Friction

Without institutionalized budgets or dedicated infrastructure, CMMA’s daily operations rely
almost entirely on relational capital and informal lobbying. In her interview, Sukyuen explained
that accessing essential services for victims requires constant negotiation: "I get my assistance
setup, and reiterate: We cannot leave these people who've been through severe trauma walking
around... It takes a lot of informal meetings and coffees with the right people. That is the way we
lobby. We lobby around... You put your head down and you hustle and network."

This informal hustle operates against three systemic administrative bottlenecks:

   ●​ Civil Registration (CENSO): Registering a trafficking victim with the civil registry
      (CENSO) to obtain formal documentation is essential for legal employment and peace of
      mind. However, registration involves months of bureaucratic friction, limited appointment
      slots, and registry staff who are unfamiliar with trafficking protocols, often requiring four
      or five attempts to process a single case.​

   ●​ Healthcare Access (AZV): Aruba’s national health insurance (AZV) is legally restricted
      to individuals with valid legal residence or Dutch nationality. Undocumented victims and
      those on temporary reflection periods cannot access routine medical care without
      exceptional bureaucratic intervention. Specialized medical care, such as OB-GYN
      services for female trafficking victims, requires filing special administrative appeals and
      waiting three to five months.​

   ●​ Psychological and Mental Health Care: Formal mental health institutions (such as
      Respaldo) require AZV coverage and cannot legally admit undocumented migrants
      without specialized billing arrangements. Because no institutionalized trauma care exists
      for undocumented victims, Sukyuen, who holds a background in social work and a minor
      in psychology, is forced to informally provide crisis coaching, de-escalation, and basic
      therapy herself.​

   ●​ Staffing Insecurity: Sukyuen’s own employment contract with the government
      remained temporary and unresolved throughout early 2026. Because CMMA consists of
      only two people, the potential loss of the victim assistance coordinator represents an
      existential threat: without her, the entire victim assistance pillar of the Aruban
      government ceases to function.


## 5. The Visibility Paradox: Empirical Analysis of

### 5.2 The Mechanics of the Paradox

The fact that zero Venezuelan victims were officially identified in 2025, despite Venezuelans
comprising the overwhelming majority of displaced persons on the island (roughly 17,000 in a
population of 110,000, or 159 per 1,000 residents), is the clearest empirical evidence of the


visibility paradox. The operational logic of this paradox becomes clear when contrasted with
regional regularizations, such as Colombia's Permiso Especial de Permanencia (PEP). In
Colombia, granting temporary legal status reduced the fear of deportation, which led directly to
a measurable increase in reported trafficking and exploitation cases among Venezuelan women.
Legal protection did not create more harm; it made existing victims safe enough to approach the
state and enter the official record.

In Aruba, the mechanism runs in reverse. Because no European or domestic asylum framework
covers economic displacement, undocumented Venezuelans face immediate administrative
detention and deportation if they approach authorities. Furthermore, because many
Venezuelans are long-settled and supported by informal diaspora networks, they rarely have the
acute, disruptive contact with police that triggers formal identification. The resulting official data
showing zero Venezuelan victims is an artifact of institutional design: the legal system
suppresses the very conditions under which victims could safely step forward.


## 6. Discursive Formations, Educational Outreach, and

      Positionality

### 6.1 Challenging the Spectacle of the Other: Bo ta conocemi?

Stuart Hall’s analysis of representation and the "spectacle of the Other" highlights how public
discourse constructs racialized migrant bodies as threats, economic burdens, or criminals. In
Aruba, public debate frequently blames Venezuelan migrants for deteriorating infrastructure,
overcrowded emergency rooms, and wage suppression. During my fieldwork with CMMA, I
contributed to the production of a four-part educational short-form video campaign titled Bo ta
conocemi? ("Do you know me?"). The campaign was designed for Aruban youth in secondary
schools and young adults (ages 12-25).

The educational campaign operates through a tripartite pedagogical method: starting with a
widespread public statement, introducing a critical question to disrupt the premise, and
concluding with a factual correction grounded in verified institutional data. For example, the
campaign directly confronts the popular assumption that Venezuelans enter the island
irregularly simply to undercut local wages. The video questions why an individual would borrow
hundreds of dollars and risk their life on an overloaded boat at night if safe avenues existed,
before presenting the factual correction: over 98 percent of arrivals flee basic economic and
medical collapse, and formal visa pathways have been closed since 2019.

Across its four thematic modules, the campaign systematically breaks down misconceptions
regarding irregular crossings, explains the legal trap created by narrow asylum laws, demystifies
human trafficking as everyday debt bondage and employer control, and encourages young
Arubans to direct their frustration toward institutional planning deficits rather than the vulnerable
individuals navigating them.



### 6.2 The Policy Dilemma: The Pull-Factor Constraint

Developing this educational material revealed an acute policy contradiction. Government
stakeholders expressed concern that distributing accurate, multilingual information regarding
available victim protections on open social media platforms (like TikTok or Instagram) could
inadvertently act as an advertisement or "pull factor" in Venezuela, encouraging further irregular
boat crossings. As a result, the campaign was restricted to structured, closed educational
environments (such as high school classrooms and workshops) rather than open digital
broadcasting. This restriction exemplifies how state anxieties over border control actively
constrain public human rights education.


### 6.3 Methodology, Positionality, and Limitations

This study is methodologically grounded in 40 hours of volunteer service and qualitative
fieldwork conducted between January and February 2026 at CMMA’s offices in Oranjestad and
remotely from Leeuwarden. The primary data includes structured interviews using the McKinsey
7-S framework with Director Jeanette Richardson and Victim Assistance Coordinator Sukyuen.

It is essential to acknowledge the methodological limitations of this approach. Leaning heavily
on qualitative interviews with the only two staff members of an agency introduces inherent
institutional bias: both practitioners possess a clear professional stake in arguing that their
precarity is caused by political deprioritization rather than genuine macroeconomic scarcity.

To mitigate this subjectivity, their personal accounts were systematically triangulated against
external documentary evidence:

   ●​ The Aruban government’s official 2026 TIP Report questionnaire submission.​

   ●​ Quantitative law enforcement, referral, and assistance datasets from the Court of First
      Instance and the Unit for Human Trafficking and Migrant Smuggling (UMM).​

   ●​ Multi-year Kingdom budget allocations under Project Versterking Grenstoezicht.​

   ●​ Independent international monitoring reports from the Council of Europe (GRETA),
      UNHCR/R4V tracking platforms, and Amnesty International.​




## 7. Conclusion

The institutional precarity of CMMA is not an isolated administrative failure. It is the predictable
outcome of an asymmetric constitutional architecture that separates European security interests
from European protection obligations. The Kingdom of the Netherlands projects hard sovereign
power into the Caribbean through economic pressures, naval patrols, radar infrastructure, and
Coast Guard interdiction, yet it treats the humanitarian fallout of regional displacement as a
strictly local, Aruban concern. When geopolitical shocks like the January 2026 U.S. intervention
in Venezuela occur, they destabilize an already fragile system. Frontline agencies are left to


absorb increasing displacement pressures while their international NGO funding is severed,
their Kingdom grants expire, and their national budget allocations are deferred. The resulting
visibility paradox, where official metrics record zero Venezuelan trafficking victims in an island
with the highest displacement density in the world, demonstrates that absence in administrative
data is not absence of harm. It is evidence of a system built to manage borders rather than
protect people.

Resolving this priority misalignment requires moving beyond ad-hoc grants and informal
practitioner lobbying. If the Kingdom of the Netherlands and the European Union intend to treat
anti-trafficking commitments as genuine human rights imperatives, they must bridge the OCT
protection gap. That requires extending baseline, structural financing to frontline protection
agencies and establishing subsidiary humanitarian protection standards that reflect the realities
of modern displacement. Until that structural gap is closed, frontline practitioners like Jeanette
and Sukyuen will continue to carry the human weight of global geopolitics on their own
shoulders, and people will continue to suffer under the political inertia this creates.
